# Justice in Ancient Greece

"If you knew your history,  
Then you'd know where I'm coming from." -- Bob Marley

"If you don't know your past  
You don't know your future" -- Ziggy Marley 

Why should we bother exploring what some people, who lived in a land an ocean
away from us, and millennia before our time, thought about an idea like justice? 

One answer is, "Because the past lives on in us today."  Is that the best
answer? I don't know. But I do think it is an answer sufficient to justify our
interest, if, for some reason, we do not find sheer fascination with the story
of those people to be enough on its own. 

In our case, we are going to examine transformations in the concept of justice
that occurred over several centuries on the peninsula of Greece, roughly between
about 800 B.C. and 350 B.C. This transformation is organically tied to the
growth of what we might call "Greek civilization." So perhaps we should pause
here and ask what we mean by a "civilization."

One way to define "civilization" is an ideal type, describing a society
organized in a significantly more complex arrangement than the tribe or clan,
such that it produces large-scale evidence of that complexity. (There are
certainly other existing as well as possible definitions of the term
civilization. We will employ the definition above because we find it aids our
theorizing.) Those productions which we take to be the distinctive marks of a
civilization may include monumental architecture, enduring artworks such as
statuary, elaborate craftwork, coinage, large-scale agriculture, writing,
bureaucracy, formalized law systems, and more.

This definition makes no claim that "civilized" people are somehow better or
more valuable than "savage" people. It makes no claim that *a society* we
recognize as civilized is necessarily better than one organized around the tribe
or clan. (Perhaps being civilized is a true advance over pre-civilized living,
or perhaps it actually binds us in chains, as Rousseau suggested. Here, we
merely bracket that question, as something we might consider on another day.) We
will use "civilized" simply to signify a notable difference in social
arrangements, a difference worthy of exploration. 

In any case, whether for better or for worse, the rise of a civilization in some
part of the human world, at some point in time, represents the efflorescence of
a noteworthy level of societal complexity. Thus, I contend, it is both
appropriate and useful for us, living in an almost "hyper-civilized" society, to
study these episodes with particular attention, in the interest of seeing how we
arrived at where we are.

(And here I will note that while the true historical spirit is moved chiefly by
the simple delight of determining what were the "goings-on" that occurred at
some time in the past, examining the patterns we might come to recognize in
those goings-on as aids to understanding our current situation is also a worthy
undertaking.)

In a traditional society, although notable events may periodically occur, such
as an episode when the chief was gored by an angry boar, there are no social
transformations that introduce significant discontinuities within those people's
social life. That is because, per the ideal type we will employ of a
"traditional society," if there were, that society would cease to be strictly
traditional, and would begin to take on, at least to some extent, that condition
which we call "modernity."

Now I take the condition of modernity itself to be a matter of degree, in that
the changes that occur as a society becomes "more modern" can still be
explained historically: we are not forced to regard them as akin to a phase
change, such as when liquid water suddenly becomes frozen. In this
understanding, what we might call the "modern era," which we could date,
depending on our theoretical predilections, as beginning somewhere between the
1400s and the 1600s, is not one in which the condition of modernity emerges,
fully developed, like Athena from the head of Zeus, without any gestation period
beyond a brief, splitting headache.

![The birth of Athena from the head of Zeus, Attic black-figure amphora, c. 550&ndash;525 B.C., Louvre (F 32). Rather, the modern age is a period in which the intensification of that
condition has proceeded to an extent that some of those experiencing it begin to
posit "becoming more modern" not merely as a problematic process seeking
explanation, but as a virtuous activity, which thus ought to be deliberately
pursued. To the extent that such voices have gained influence in a society, that
society itself has begun to pursue a process of "modernization." We can see such
a process pursued quite explicitly in, for example, Kemal Ataturk's
modernization program for Turkey, Reza Shah's similar undertaking in Persia
(which included changing the country's name to Iran), or Mao's even more
"vigorous" efforts in China. (In both Turkey and Iran, persuading the population
to modernize employed fair amounts of violence, but Mao's thuggery made
Ataturk's and Shah's brutality appear but as a soft nudge.)

![Reza Shah at his coronation, Tehran, April 25, 1926. [Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Reza_shah_coronation.jpg).](../images/reza-shah-coronation.jpg){alt="Reza Shah in coronation robes and plumed crown, standing before a throne"}

Modernity is problematic for the people who experience it, in a way that the
activities of a traditional society are not for its members. Since those entering into
this condition have cast off the way of their ancestors, and are aware that they
have done so, on what basis do they justify how they are now behaving? Why is
that way better than any other way they might conduct their lives? More
specifically, why is their new way of doing things to be preferred to the
traditional way that they have abandoned? 

Of course, traditional societies themselves are not fixed in amber, like the
fossil of some ancient insect. They change, for instance, by adopting a new way
of making spearheads, discovering a new source of food, or crossing some
mountains and moving into a new valley. But those changes are not so disruptive
that they prompt the people undergoing them to ask, "Why are we now living in
this way, rather than in some alternative fashion, one of which we possess some
significant awareness?"

And once again, it is worth pointing out that this distinction follows from the
ideal types we are using. We are not attempting to identify an historical fact,
asserting that we have examined all "traditional" societies, and found that none
of them asked such a question. That effort would itself assume some prior
characterization of what is meant by a "traditional society." In terms of the
ideal types we are employing, any society that began to ask such questions would
be less and less characterized by the ideal type "traditional society," and more
and more conformant to the ideal type "modern society." 

The first symptoms of modernity can be recognized when some people begin to
formulate myths that act as explanations, not merely for their existence in the
cosmos, but for their existence as a different form of society from those around
them. We can see this type of explanation arising, for instance, in Egyptian
mythology that explains the preeminence of the pharaoh, or in the Old Testament,
where the Hebrews offer explanations as to how they were different from the
people around them, or in Greek myths that told of how the more conceptually
characterized Olympian gods overthrew the more elemental gods that had preceded
them.

And that brings us back to ancient Greece. In the progress of that society, in
the three texts we will read -- the *Iliad*, *Antigone*, and the *Nicomachean
Ethics* -- we see attempts to grapple with the question of how one should live
justly. The latter attempts build upon the previous ones, and rather than
denying the earlier concepts of justice, they build upon them.

Here, it is worth pointing out that there is no attempt to "pitch" any of these
views to you, as if we could solve many of America's social problems by simply
becoming Homeric heroes or fifth-century Athenians.

But what is possible, through studying these texts, is coming to understand that
different conceptions of justice, each of them reasonable in its own way, can
and do co-exist. And I hope that you will see that these conceptions have their
echoes in the world around us today.
